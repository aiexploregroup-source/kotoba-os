# Japanese Learning Resources — Complete Study and Analysis Report

**Prepared:** 2026-11-21  
**Scope:** The curated apps, websites, YouTube channels, books, tutors, and learning stacks supplied by the user, plus closely related official/free resources.  
**Important:** This list is curated, not exhaustive. Prices and plan terms are **indicative** and were checked from public sources on 2026-11-21 where possible. Always verify on the provider’s own site before paying. Exchange rates, taxes, regional pricing, promotions, and subscription terms can change.

---

## 1. Executive Summary

Japanese learning resources are not interchangeable tools. They sit in different layers of a complete system:

1. **Foundations:** kana, pronunciation, basic sentence patterns.
2. **Core instruction:** a structured beginner course or textbook.
3. **Memory systems:** spaced repetition for kanji, vocabulary, and grammar.
4. **Comprehensible input:** graded listening and reading.
5. **Output practice:** speaking and writing with feedback.
6. **Reference:** dictionaries, grammar guides, and searchable examples.
7. **Exam or proficiency track:** JLPT, textbook progression, CEFR-style goals.
8. **Immersion:** authentic media, native video, news, books, games, and conversation.

No single app covers all layers well.

### Best overall conclusions

- **Best free foundation:** Tofugu kana guide + NHK Easy Japanese + Tae Kim or Wasabi + Anki/Jpdb + Jisho + selected YouTube lessons.
- **Best low-cost all-in-one practice app:** **Renshuu**. It is unusually broad and has a genuinely useful free tier.
- **Best paid kanji system:** **WaniKani**. Excellent for kanji and vocabulary through radicals, mnemonics, and SRS, but it does not teach grammar, speaking, or listening.
- **Best paid grammar SRS:** **Bunpro**. Strong for JLPT-oriented grammar, examples, and sentence-level review.
- **Best structured app-only beginner course:** **LingoDeer**. Better grammar explanation than Duolingo and more Japan-specific than many general apps, but it does not carry the learner all the way to advanced fluency.
- **Best speaking-first audio course:** **Pimsleur**, especially for building automatic question-and-answer speech habits. It is weak for reading and kanji.
- **Best human correction:** **italki** or **Preply**. Tutor feedback is the fastest way to find pronunciation, phrasing, and usage errors that apps miss.
- **Best textbook spine:** **Genki** for a friendly Western-style start, **Minna no Nihongo** for a rigorous classroom-style start, **Tobira** or **Quartet** for intermediate transition.
- **Best free institution-grade content:** **Japan Foundation Minato**, **Irodori**, **Erin’s Challenge**, **NHK Easy Japanese**, **NHK News Web Easy**, **JLPT official samples**, and **FSI Japanese**.
- **Best YouTube usage:** Use YouTube as a support layer, not as the whole course. English-taught channels are good for explanation; Japanese-taught channels are better for listening and thinking in Japanese.

### Main warning

The biggest mistake is paying for several tools that do the same job. Do not stack WaniKani, Renshuu Pro, Kanshudo Pro, Kanji Recognition methods, and multiple kanji apps at once. Choose **one primary kanji/vocabulary memory system**, **one grammar spine**, **one main input source**, and **one output/feedback channel**.

This report combines:

- Public pricing and feature pages from official providers where available.

- This report evaluates learning value, not merely feature count.

### 4.2 Duolingo

**Indicative price:** Core Japanese course free with ads/Energy mechanics. Super about **$12.99/month** or **$83.99/year** in the US; about **¥2,190/month** or **¥11,900/year** on the Japanese web plan. Prices vary by platform and region.  
**Official reference:** `duolingo.com/super`

**Strengths**
- Extremely easy to start.
- Excellent habit formation.
- Good for kana familiarity, basic vocabulary, sentence pattern recognition, and daily consistency.
- Gamification helps absolute beginners who struggle to study regularly.

**Weaknesses**
- Grammar explanations are limited.
- Kanji teaching is shallow relative to WaniKani, Anki, RTK, or Kodansha.
- Speaking practice is not sufficient for real conversation.
- Listening is scripted and unnatural compared with podcasts, tutors, or authentic video.
- The Japanese course is expanding, including CEFR-oriented development reported for 2026, but it should not be treated as a complete path to B2.

**Best use**
Use Duolingo as a **daily warm-up**, not as the main course. Five to ten minutes is fine. Do not let streaks replace real study.

**Verdict**
Good for starting and habit. Insufficient as a complete Japanese course.

### 4.3 Busuu

**Indicative price:** Free A1–B2 content with ads/community features. Premium about **$12.49/month** or **$72.99/year**. Premium Plus about **$14.90/month** or **$87.90/year**.  
**Official reference:** `busuu.com/en/premium-plans`

**Strengths**
- More structured than Duolingo.
- Community feedback can help with writing and speaking.
- Premium adds AI conversations, speaking practice, mistake repair, specialty courses, vocabulary review, grammar review, streak repair, no ads, and lesson-skip features.

**Weaknesses**
- Japanese-specific AI conversation and pronunciation support may be less mature than English or Spanish.
- Not as strong as LingoDeer for Japanese grammar detail.
- Not a dedicated kanji or SRS powerhouse.
- Community corrections vary in quality.

**Best use**
A reasonable guided app for beginners who want a social element. Pair with WaniKani/Anki and native listening.

**Verdict**
Useful structured app, but not a complete ecosystem.

### 4.4 LingoDeer

**Indicative price:** About **$14.99/month**, **$39.99/quarter**, **$95.99/year**, **$189.99/3 years**. A 6-year plan was also listed at about **$239.99** in the checked pricing page. Free content includes alphabet/Unit 1, Test Outs, and Travel Phrasebook.  
**Official reference:** `lingodeer.com/pricing`

**Strengths**
- One of the best app-only courses for Japanese.
- Clear grammar explanations.
- Good native audio.
- Stronger script and writing treatment than Duolingo.
- Useful structure from A1 toward around B1, depending on how content is counted.

**Weaknesses**
- Limited free content.
- Paid plans can become expensive if bought monthly.
- Does not carry the learner into advanced reading, immersion, or spontaneous conversation.
- Review system is useful but less configurable than Anki.

**Best use**
As a structured beginner-to-lower-intermediate course, especially for learners who dislike textbooks.

**Verdict**
Strong paid app choice. Better than Duolingo as a primary course, but should still be paired with input and output practice.

### 4.5 JapanesePod101

**Indicative price:** Free plan with first 3 lessons in every pathway, 50+ survival phrases, vocabulary lists, and weekly lessons. Basic about **$4/month** on a 24-month plan or **$8** month-to-month. Premium about **$10/month** on a 24-month plan or **$25** month-to-month. Premium PLUS about **$23/month** on a 24-month plan or **$47** month-to-month. Promotions were visible at the checked date.  
**Official reference:** `japanesepod101.com/pricing`

**Strengths**
- Huge audio/video lesson library.
- Excellent for listening, phrases, cultural context, and repeated exposure.
- Lesson notes are useful.
- Good for commuters and auditory learners.
- Multiple levels and series.

**Weaknesses**
- Low advertised monthly rates usually require long commitments.
- Large catalog can feel unstructured.
- Lessons often include English scaffolding, which can slow transition to native-speed comprehension.
- Kanji and productive grammar practice need a dedicated companion.
- Premium PLUS tutor/teacher features depend on usage and availability.

**Best use**
Use as an input library and listening bridge. Pick a pathway and follow it; do not randomly browse forever.

**Verdict**
Very useful for listening and phrase acquisition. Not sufficient alone for reading, kanji, or grammar precision.

### 4.6 Renshuu

**Indicative price:** Free tier with no time limit and no ads, though with fewer quiz types. Pro about **$6.99/month**, roughly **$49.99–$59.99/year**, and about **$109.99–$129.99/lifetime** depending on promotion/region.  
**Reference:** official app/site plus independent 2026 comparison.

**Strengths**
- Extremely broad: vocabulary, kanji, grammar, JLPT, textbook-aligned paths, worksheets, radicals, mnemonics, tracing, and quizzes.
- Free tier is genuinely useful.
- No-ads free access is rare.
- Good for Genki and Minna no Nihongo learners.
- Pro can be annual or lifetime rather than only subscription.

**Weaknesses**
- Dense and highly configurable interface.
- Requires internet.
- Speaking is not a strength.
- Free tier lacks some listening, pitch, writing, and sentence-context question types.
- Learner must build or choose a path.

**Best use**
As the best free/low-cost all-in-one practice hub. Use it alongside a main course or as the main practice engine for disciplined learners.

**Verdict**
Excellent value. One of the highest-value tools in the whole list.

### 4.7 WaniKani

**Indicative price:** Free levels 1–3. Premium about **$9/month**, **$89/year**, or **$299/lifetime**.  
**Official reference:** `knowledge.wanikani.com/.../wanikani-premium-membership`

**Strengths**
- Best-in-class kanji acquisition system for many learners.
- Radical-based decomposition and mnemonics.
- Strong SRS scheduling.
- Teaches about 2,000 kanji and 6,000 words.
- Clear level progression and community support.

**Weaknesses**
- No grammar, speaking, or listening.
- Fixed order; cannot skip freely.
- Mnemonics can feel slow or silly for some learners.
- Meaning-first focus does not automatically produce recall of all readings in spontaneous use.
- Reviews can become a daily obligation.

**Best use**
Use 15–30 minutes daily. Pair with grammar study and reading so kanji become usable rather than merely recognized.

**Verdict**
Excellent if kanji is a bottleneck. Do not buy it if you only want conversation and never intend to read Japanese.

### 4.8 Bunpro

**Indicative price:** Free limited access. 30-day Premium trial with no credit card required. Premium about **$5/month**. Lifetime about **$150**. Annual pricing was reported around **$50/year** in independent research.  
**Official reference:** `bunpro.jp/pricing`

**Strengths**
- 900+ grammar points.
- 10,000+ example sentences.
- Native audio.
- JLPT N5–N1 organization.
- SRS grammar review, ghost reviews, decks, and WaniKani integration.
- References to textbooks and external resources.

**Weaknesses**
- Sentence-level recognition is not the same as spontaneous production.
- Best results require speaking/writing practice outside the app.
- Beginners may need a gentler explanation source first.
- Too many reviews can accumulate if ignored.

**Best use**
As the grammar review engine after or alongside Tae Kim, Wasabi, Genki, or textbook study.

**Verdict**
One of the best paid grammar tools, especially for JLPT learners.

### 4.9 Anki

**Indicative price:** Free on desktop and Android; AnkiWeb sync is free. **AnkiMobile for iOS about $24.99 one-time** in the US.  
**Official reference:** `apps.ankiweb.net`

**Strengths**
- Extremely powerful SRS.
- FSRS and customizable scheduling.
- Handles 100,000+ cards.
- Audio, images, video, and rich formatting.
- Huge shared-deck ecosystem.
- Full user control.

**Weaknesses**
- Setup burden is high.
- Shared decks vary wildly in quality.
- Easy to overload with reviews.
- No built-in curriculum.
- Can become a card-making hobby instead of language learning.

**Best use**
Use Anki for vocabulary, sentence cards, textbook decks, and anything you personally need to remember. Keep card creation simple.

**Verdict**
Best customizable memory tool. Worst out-of-box course. Ideal for self-directed learners.

**Official reference:** `jpdb.io`

**Strengths**
- Excellent vocabulary and kanji extraction from text.

### 4.11 Kanshudo

**Indicative price:** Free capped access. Pro about **$9/month** or **$90/year**.  
**Official reference:** `kanshudo.com/faq`

**Strengths**
- Very large database: about 14,000 kanji, 260,000 words, 800 grammar points, and 150,000 examples.
- Personalized weak-point study.
- Combines dictionary, grammar reference, games, and AI tutor features.
- Useful for kanji depth and search.

**Weaknesses**
- Web-centered.
- Interface can feel busy.
- Overlaps heavily with WaniKani, Anki, Jpdb, and Bunpro.
- Pro is less compelling if already paying for two dedicated SRS tools.

**Best use**
As a research/personalization hub rather than a second kanji SRS.

**Verdict**
Powerful but overlapping. Choose it instead of—not in addition to—another paid kanji system unless you have a specific need.

### 6.2 Japanese-taught/comprehensible input channels

| Channel | Best for | Caution |
|---|---|---|

| Miku Real Japanese | Natural Japanese, conversation, Japanese-only teaching | Better after basic grammar. |

| Comprehensible Japanese | Beginner comprehensible input | Excellent for listening-building with context. |
| Japanese Time | Natural Japanese, culture, conversational listening | Use as immersion rather than systematic grammar. |

### How to use YouTube effectively

1. Pick **one main channel**, not ten.
2. Watch with a purpose: grammar point, shadowing, or listening comprehension.
3. Pause and repeat short segments.
4. Write 3–5 example sentences.
5. Put useful sentences into Anki or Jpdb.
6. Say the sentences aloud.
7. Re-watch after one week.

Passive binge-watching creates the feeling of learning without durable acquisition.

- Budget learner: use tutors for corrections, not as the entire course.

| Learning need | Strongly covered by | Common gap | Recommendation |

| Kana | Tofugu, NHK Easy, LingoDeer, Duolingo | Learners recognize but do not instantly read | Drill reading until automatic; do not rush. |
| Basic grammar | Tae Kim, Wasabi, Genki, LingoDeer, YouTube | Too many explanations, too little practice | Choose one grammar spine; use others only to clarify. |
| Advanced grammar | Imabi, Bunpro, Tobira, Quartet | Dense theory without output | Apply each point in writing and speaking. |
| Kanji recognition | WaniKani, Anki, RTK, Kanshudo, Jpdb | Recognition without reading context | Read graded text daily. |
| Kanji writing | RTK, Kodansha, Renshuu tracing | Most apps do not teach handwriting | Decide early whether handwriting is a goal. |
| Vocabulary | Anki, Jpdb, Renshuu, WaniKani, Drops | Isolated words not usable | Study sentence cards and read. |
| Listening | JapanesePod101, Pimsleur, NHK, FluentU, Lingopie, YouTube | Scripted listening does not equal native-speed comprehension | Mix scripted and authentic input. |
| Speaking | Pimsleur, Speechling, tutors, HelloTalk, Tandem | Few chances for real feedback | Schedule output weekly. |
| Writing correction | tutors, HelloTalk, Tandem, Lang-8 if available | Apps rarely correct nuanced Japanese | Get human correction on short texts. |
| Pitch accent | Dogen, Pimsleur, coach feedback | Overemphasis can distract beginners | Start light; increase focus after N5/N4. |
| Reading | NHK Easy, Matcha, Satori Reader, graded readers | Learners avoid text because it is slow | Read 10–20 minutes daily. |
| JLPT | Bunpro, WaniKani, JLPT samples, Nihongo no Mori | Exam prep can become test-taking only | Keep speaking and reading alongside exam study. |

## 10. Practical Study Roadmap

### Phase 0 — Setup, days 1–3

- Choose one main course.
- Choose one SRS.
- Install Jisho/Japandict/Jotoba bookmarks.
- Create an Anki or Jpdb workflow.
- Pick one YouTube channel.
- Decide whether handwriting is a goal.

### Phase 1 — Kana and survival Japanese, weeks 1–4

**Goal:** read kana automatically and understand basic greetings/phrases.

Resources:
- Tofugu kana or LingoDeer alphabet.
- NHK Easy Japanese lessons.
- Duolingo or Drops for habit if motivating.
- Pimsleur Lesson 1 / free trial to test speaking-first style.

Milestones:
- Hiragana and katakana recognition above 90%.
- 100–200 useful words.
- Can introduce yourself and ask simple questions.

### Phase 2 — Beginner core, months 2–6

**Goal:** complete a real beginner course and establish SRS.

Resources:
- Genki I, LingoDeer, or Minna no Nihongo.
- WaniKani/Renshuu/Anki for kanji and vocabulary.
- Tae Kim or Wasabi for grammar clarification.
- JapanesePod101 for extra listening.
- r/LearnJapanese for questions.

Milestones:
- 500–1,000 words.
- 100–300 kanji depending on method.
- Basic N5-level grammar.
- Can read NHK Easy slowly.
- Can hold simple scripted conversations.

### Phase 3 — Late beginner / N4, months 6–12

**Goal:** move from textbook sentences to real input.

Resources:
- Genki II or equivalent.
- Bunpro N5/N4.
- Satori Reader, NHK News Web Easy, Matcha.
- Nihongo no Mori or ToKini Andy.
- italki/Preply weekly.

Milestones:
- 1,500–3,000 words.
- 500+ kanji.
- Can read short native-ish texts with lookups.
- Can discuss daily topics for 10–15 minutes.

### Phase 4 — Intermediate / N3, months 12–24

**Goal:** handle authentic material with effort.

Resources:
- Tobira or Quartet.
- Bunpro N3.
- Jpdb-based immersion.
- FluentU/Lingopie if useful.
- Dogen for pronunciation refinement.
- Tutor discussions.

Milestones:
- 5,000+ words.
- 1,000+ kanji.
- Can watch beginner/intermediate native video with partial comprehension.
- Can write short essays and receive correction.

### Phase 5 — Upper intermediate and beyond

**Goal:** broaden vocabulary, register, and spontaneous expression.

Resources:
- Native news, essays, fiction, podcasts, games.
- Imabi/advanced grammar references.
- Frequent tutor or exchange.
- Specialized reading and domain vocabulary.

Milestones:
- Read native material daily.
- Discuss abstract topics.
- Notice and correct your own recurring errors.
- Use Japanese for real purposes, not only study.

These timelines are estimates. Intensity, prior language experience, and study quality matter more than calendar time.

## 11. What to Buy First — Decision Guide

### If you are an absolute beginner

Buy nothing at first. Use:
- NHK Easy Japanese
- Tofugu kana
- Tae Kim or Wasabi
- Anki/Jpdb
- Jisho

After 2–4 weeks, choose a structured course.

### If you need structure

Choose:
- **LingoDeer** for app-only structure.
- **Genki** for textbook structure.
- **JapanesePod101** for audio/video structure.
- **Minna no Nihongo** if you have or want a teacher.

### If kanji is your main problem

Choose one:
- **WaniKani** for guided SRS and mnemonics.
- **Renshuu** for cheaper all-in-one practice.
- **RTK/Kodansha** if you like books and handwriting.

Do not buy all three at once.

### If grammar is your main problem

Choose:
- **Tae Kim** for free concise logic.
- **Wasabi** for beginner-friendly explanation.
- **Bunpro** for SRS grammar and JLPT structure.
- **Imabi** for exhaustive reference.

### If speaking is your main problem

Choose:
- **Pimsleur** for structured audio habit.
- **Speechling** for pronunciation drills.
- **italki/Preply** for real feedback.

### If reading is your main problem

Choose:
- **NHK News Web Easy + Matcha** free.
- **Satori Reader** paid.
- **Jpdb** for vocabulary from content.

### If you are preparing for JLPT

Choose:
- **Bunpro**
- **WaniKani or Jpdb**
- **official JLPT samples**
- **Nihongo no Mori**

## 12. Things Not to Spend Money On Too Early

1. **Lifetime plans** before confirming you like the method.
2. **Multiple overlapping kanji apps.**
3. **Expensive specialty courses** like $297 programs before finishing a beginner course.
4. **Dictionary apps** when free web dictionaries are enough.
5. **Pitch-accent obsession** before basic kana, vocabulary, and listening are stable.
6. **Immersive video subscriptions** before you can understand enough to learn from them.
7. **Too many shared Anki decks**, which can create review debt.
8. **Every YouTube channel**, which creates content consumption rather than acquisition.

## 15. Final Verdict

The strongest learning system from your curated list is not a single product. It is a deliberate combination:

- **Course/structure:** Genki, LingoDeer, JapanesePod101, or NHK Easy.
- **Grammar:** Tae Kim/Wasabi plus Bunpro.
- **Kanji/vocabulary:** WaniKani or Renshuu, with Anki/Jpdb for personalization.
- **Input:** NHK Easy, NHK News Web Easy, Erin’s Challenge, Satori Reader, Comprehensible Japanese.
- **Output:** italki/Preply, Speechling, Pimsleur, HelloTalk/Tandem.
- **Reference:** Jisho, Japandict, Jotoba, Imabi.
- **Immersion:** YouTube, native media, Matcha, FluentU/Lingopie if useful.

### Best default recommendation

For most learners in 2026:

1. Start free with **Tofugu kana + NHK Easy + Tae Kim/Wasabi + Anki/Jpdb + Jisho**.
2. After 2–4 weeks, choose one paid engine:
   - **Renshuu Pro** for all-in-one value.
   - **WaniKani + Bunpro** for the strongest split kanji/grammar system.
   - **LingoDeer** for app-based structure.
3. Add **one tutor lesson every 1–2 weeks** as soon as you can form simple sentences.
4. Read something in Japanese every day.
5. Keep YouTube as support, not the core curriculum.

That combination covers every important layer without unnecessary spending.
